Further Study Is Needed

Often, near the end of a session, we've gone through the presentation or the poster, and (because I'm not a biochemist or a zoologist) I ask some version of “so what did you actually find?” There's usually a pause, and then the person tells me, often strugglingly, what their work revealed. (Less often, they tell me why I should care — but that's another, different step.)

And then I often hear, or have already read, “But of course, further study is needed.”

In 1854, as cholera, from one particular well, killed Londoners in Soho, the physician John Snow did something few scientists of his day would have dared. He did not write that his research “suggested a possible association between the Broad Street pump and elevated mortality, though further study is warranted.” He walked to the parish officials and told them to “take the handle off the pump.” They did. The deaths stopped.

I think about that pump handle often, because sometimes the researchers I work with have a good version of it in their own papers — but they bury it.

I've learned to be careful, because in Japan especially, understatement isn't weakness; it's professionalism and respect for your colleagues. And often, of course, more research IS needed. I don't want my researchers to sound like an American salesman at a podium.

And yet there is a difference between humility and self-erasure, and the reader on the far side of an international journal can't always tell which one they're looking at. A Western reviewer may hear “the author isn't sure,” and a tired editor may hear nothing at all, and move on. The finding — the pump handle — may disappear completely.

It helps me to ask this: “If you had to name the one thing you're fairly sure of — not certain, just fairly sure — what would it be?” And they say it. And I ask whether we might put that sentence somewhere a hurried reader will actually find it. Usually they can. Sometimes they're surprised it's allowed.

I don't know whether John Snow was brave or simply impatient. But the handle came off in 1854 because he let the finding out from under all the caution and said it where it could be heard. Most of the researchers I meet are quite able to do this. My work, on the good days, is to be in the room to empower this in them.
