What Do You Work On?

A researcher recently told me the hardest part of a conference wasn't her talk. It was the coffee breaks. Someone would turn to her, badge to badge, and ask, “So, what do you work on?” She'd flown across the Pacific for a three day conference, but the hard part was the small talk.

But besides being an opener to meet new colleagues, the question What do you work on? is sometimes really, really important.

Serendipity: in other words, a lucky coincidence.

In 2011, at a conference in Puerto Rico, Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna were introduced over coffee. They got talking about their work, and it turned out they were studying the same bacterial enzyme from opposite directions. They wound up collaborating; the result was CRISPR gene editing, and nine years later it meant a Nobel Prize for both women. It happened because two people could tell each other, plainly, what they were focused on.

Recombinant DNA, the beginning of modern biotechnology, reportedly started the same way, over corned-beef sandwiches at a deli near Waikiki! Two researchers at a 1972 conference got talking and realized one had the very tool the other needed.

This is what I try to get across to the researchers I work with. Of course, networking is vital these days. But the short summary of your work is also a signal, and somewhere, at some conference, you might meet an important collaborator. If you can say what you do in simple terms, it could happen. It could even be via a third party. “Oh, bacterial enzymes? You should talk to Jenny Doudna!”

So I help researchers get ready for the coffee break. So many good things that happen at a conference don't happen on the program.
