Who Are You Talking To?

It's the first question I ask every researcher I work with, before we open a single slide: who are you talking to? Many problems we hit later turn out to be that question, left unanswered.

Two people came to me around the same time.

The first arrived with a careful, chart-heavy presentation on a home-visit dental program. Some Japanese children with severe disabilities can't be brought to a clinic, so the care has to come to them. But she wasn't speaking at a conference — she was reporting to the city government. And the city didn't need her methodology or data tables. They needed to know how many children were going without care, what a home program costs, and what it saves when a small problem doesn't grow into a hospital visit. For that audience, the story was logistics and money.

The second came in with beautiful biomedical images — the thin walls of the lung, the difficult business of keeping a COVID patient breathing without damaging their lung. Perfect for a clinical conference. But he was slated to approach a company, and a company isn't moved by surfactants or blood-air barrier stats. But they might be commercially interested in a gentler ventilator that holds the lungs open with less damage — one that they could build, sell, and get into hospitals. Same science, but here it's about a product, costs, and the market. Again, not a sales pitch, but the focus is on the need and on real-world possibilities.

I couldn't critique the technical details — their colleagues and professors could do that — but I could put them in the mind-set of their listeners, what each hadn't pictured. Once you can see the people in front of you, you can eliminate what's not needed and insert what is impactful.

“It's about them, not you” sounds like a rule about manners, but it's really about focus.

And it's the same question, later, in the poster hall — except there the room is a corridor and you have three seconds. But that's still another conversation.
